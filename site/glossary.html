Total return

The overall percentage gain or loss over the whole period.

Total return is the simplest performance number: how much an investment grew or shrank over the full period, expressed as a percentage of the starting value. A total return of 50% means one dollar became one dollar and fifty cents. A total return of -20% means one dollar became eighty cents.

total_return = ending_value / starting_value - 1

What it tells you: the raw bottom line. What it hides: when the gains happened and how bumpy the ride was. A strategy that doubled your money over 30 years and one that doubled it over 3 years both show a 100% total return, even though they are very different. For that reason total return is best read alongside CAGR (which puts it on a per-year basis) and maximum drawdown (which shows the worst dip along the way).

CAGR (Compound Annual Growth Rate)

The average yearly growth rate, smoothed as if it were steady.

CAGR stands for Compound Annual Growth Rate. It answers the question: at what steady yearly rate would the investment have had to grow to get from its starting value to its ending value over the period? It converts a total return over many years into a single comparable per-year number.

CAGR = (ending_value / starting_value) ^ (1 / years) - 1

For example, growing from 100 to 200 over 10 years is a 100% total return, but a CAGR of about 7.2% per year, because 1.072 ^ 10 is roughly 2. The word compound matters: each year's growth builds on the previous year's, so CAGR is not simply the total return divided by the number of years.

Why it is useful: it lets you compare strategies or assets that ran over different lengths of time on an equal, annualized footing. What it hides: like total return, it says nothing about volatility or the worst loss along the way. Two investments can share a CAGR while one moved smoothly and the other lurched violently.

Sharpe ratio

Return earned per unit of risk. Higher is better.

The Sharpe ratio measures return per unit of risk. Two strategies might earn the same average return, but if one does it with wild swings and the other does it smoothly, the smooth one is better in a risk-adjusted sense. The Sharpe ratio captures exactly that: it divides the average return by how much the returns bounce around, then scales the result to a yearly figure.

Sharpe = (mean daily return / standard deviation of daily returns) * sqrt(252)

The sqrt(252) factor annualizes it, since there are about 252 trading days in a year. In the strictest definition you subtract a risk-free rate from the return first. This dashboard uses a risk-free rate of zero, which is a common simplification for comparing strategies against each other.

How to read it, as a rough guide:

  • Below 1: the returns are modest relative to their volatility.
  • Around 1: a solid, respectable risk-adjusted return.
  • Above 2: excellent, and worth double checking that it is not a fluke of a short or lucky sample.

Why it is the headline metric here: it rewards strategies that make money calmly and penalizes those that only make money by taking big risks. The confidence interval shown next to each Sharpe on the Overview tells you how much to trust the estimate.

Maximum drawdown

The worst peak-to-trough drop. How bad the deepest loss got.

Maximum drawdown is the largest drop from a previous high point to a following low point, before a new high is reached. If your portfolio climbed to 100, fell to 65, then later recovered, the maximum drawdown over that stretch was -35%. It is the single number that best captures "how bad did it get" and, by extension, how much pain you would have had to sit through.

drawdown at time t = value_t / (highest value up to t) - 1

Maximum drawdown is the most negative value this takes over the whole period. It is always zero or negative, and a value closer to zero is better.

Why it matters: two strategies can have the same CAGR, but the one with a shallower maximum drawdown is far easier to actually hold through a downturn. Deep drawdowns are where investors panic and sell at the bottom. A strategy is only useful if you can stay invested in it, so drawdown is a practical measure of survivability, not just a statistic.

Win rate

The fraction of periods with a positive return.

Win rate is the fraction of periods, here trading days, on which the return was positive. A win rate of 0.54 means the portfolio rose on 54% of days and fell on the rest.

What it tells you: how often the strategy is "right" in the short term. What it does not tell you: how big the wins and losses are. A strategy can win on most days with small gains yet still lose money overall if its occasional losing days are large. The reverse is also true, some excellent strategies win less than half the time but make far more on their winners than they lose on their losers. Read win rate together with total return and Sharpe, never on its own.

Confidence interval

A plausible range for a metric, not a single guess.

Any performance number computed from history is an estimate, not a certainty. If the same strategy had experienced a slightly different sequence of ups and downs, its Sharpe ratio would have come out a little different. A confidence interval expresses that uncertainty as a range rather than a single point. The 95% interval shown on the Overview means: if we resampled the history many times, about 95% of the resulting Sharpe estimates would fall inside this band.

This project builds the interval with a stationary block bootstrap. Instead of shuffling individual daily returns, which would destroy the natural streaks and momentum in markets, it resamples contiguous blocks of days. That preserves the autocorrelation, the tendency of returns to cluster, so the interval is realistic.

How to use it: when comparing two strategies, look at whether their intervals overlap. If strategy A has a higher Sharpe than strategy B but their confidence intervals overlap heavily, the difference may not be meaningful. Wide intervals are a signal to be cautious, often a sign of a short sample or very volatile returns.

Equity curve

The value of the strategy over time, indexed to 100 at the start.

An equity curve is the line showing what a strategy would have been worth over time, starting from a common base of 100. It is the most direct picture of a strategy: an upward, steady line is good, a jagged line with deep valleys is risky, and a flat line means the strategy sat in cash.

On this dashboard every equity curve is indexed to 100 at the start of the selected window, so different strategies and portfolios can be compared on the same scale regardless of their actual dollar prices. The vertical distance between two curves at any date shows how much one strategy was ahead of another up to that point. The depth of each dip is the drawdown at that moment.

Benchmark and buy and hold

The do-nothing reference every strategy is compared against.

A benchmark is a simple reference strategy that active strategies must beat to justify their trading. The natural benchmark is buy and hold: buy the portfolio on day one, hold it to the end, and never trade. It has no rules and no costs beyond the initial purchase.

Why every comparison here includes it: trading has costs, and doing nothing is free. If a rule-based strategy cannot beat buy and hold on a risk-adjusted basis, the rule is not adding value. On the Explorer, buy and hold is always drawn as a dotted grey line so you can immediately see whether a strategy actually improved on simply holding the portfolio.

Dollar cost averaging

Invest a fixed amount at regular intervals, regardless of price.

Dollar cost averaging (DCA) invests a fixed amount of money at regular intervals rather than all at once. Because a fixed dollar amount buys a variable number of shares, the schedule automatically buys more shares when prices are low and fewer when they are high. Over time this tends to give a lower average cost per share than buying a fixed number of shares each period. The term was coined by Benjamin Graham in The Intelligent Investor.

How it is measured on this dashboard: each contribution buys into an equal-weight index of the universe at that day's price, and the reported curve is money-weighted, meaning the portfolio value divided by the total cash contributed so far, indexed to 100 at the first contribution. That puts it on the same scale as the lump-sum strategies so the curves can be compared directly.

What to expect: because DCA adds money gradually, it has less exposure early on. In a steadily rising market, a lump-sum buy and hold usually finishes ahead, since the cash DCA holds back misses early gains. In a volatile or initially falling market, DCA can do better by accumulating cheaper shares. Its main strengths are behavioral: it enforces discipline and removes the need to time the market. See the benchmark entry for why buy and hold is the natural comparison.

Universe

The set of stocks a strategy is run over.

A universe is the set of securities a strategy is applied to. It can be a single stock such as AAPL, a whole sector such as sector:Information Technology, an index such as index:sp500, a user-defined theme, or the entire market. The same strategy can be run across any universe, and results are recorded separately for each one, which is why the Overview table has both a strategy column and a universe column.

Running one strategy across many universes shows where its edge is real and where it is not. A trend rule might shine in one sector and struggle in another, and comparing those rows is often more informative than a single headline number.

Frequency and smoothing

Daily detail versus weekly, monthly, or rolling-average views.

Daily prices are noisy. The frequency control lets you step back and see the shape of a series without the day-to-day jitter.

  • Daily: every trading day, the raw series.
  • Weekly (last) and Monthly (last): keep only the last value in each week or month. This thins the line while preserving the actual levels the series reached.
  • Rolling mean: replace each point with the average of the last N days. This smooths the line to reveal the trend, but note that a rolling average lags turning points and slightly dampens peaks and troughs.

Smoothing changes only how the data is displayed and summarized. It does not change the underlying prices or the dates the strategy trades on.
